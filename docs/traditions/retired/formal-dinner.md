# Formal Dinner, waiting and long tables

For most of its history Wiess ate seated, at tables, served by its own freshmen. The O-Week histories begin with the first Master, Dr. Roy Talmage, who "instituted mandatory Wiess blazers (complete with crests) for formal Sunday dinners, where freshmen served upperclassmen a family-style dinner" [@oweek-2006 p.37]. The 1991 Rules made it law: "Freshmen shall serve at seated weekday dinners on a rotating basis… Any freshman not serving at his/her appointed meal and not providing a substitute shall be fined" [@rules-1991 §VI]. In 2003, in the new building, family style was still held "once a week… served most often by fellow freshmen serving as waiters" [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]. By 2006 it was gone: "Until two years ago, Wiess had a long tradition of family-style dinners every Monday through Thursday… But with our new building and beautiful servery, family style has become a thing of the past as quicker lines and more food choices made it a less-attractive option" [@oweek-2006 p.42]. A Wiess alumnus of the 1980s said the same in 2013: Wiess kept freshman waiting "long after all other colleges had given it up, lasting until the forced advent of shared serveries destroyed any real hope of family-style dining" [@rhc 2013-03-15 friday-afternoon-follies-one-of-the-best-beanie-shots-of-all-times comment by George Webb, 21 Mar 2013]. The maintainer remembers the custom as **Formal Dinner**, when the freshmen served the upperclassmen, at long tables [@testimony-mullen-2026-10-05c Formal Dinner] [T]. Two pieces of table manners outlived it: **cornering**, pulling up a chair at the corner of a full table, which the glossaries define from 2003 to 2025, and, according to the maintainer, the Commons' square tables.

## Cornering

"**Corner.** 1. To pull up an extra chair at the corner of a table. Frequently occurs during meals, but never at formal occasions" is in the first New Wiess glossary [@oweek-2003 conclusions p.3], and the entry ran almost unchanged for twenty-two years. "Never at formal occasions" softened to "not at formal occasions" in 2015 [@oweek-2015 p.118] and was dropped in 2021, leaving "To pull up an extra chair at the corner of a table. Frequently occurs during meals" [@oweek-2021 p.18] [@oweek-2025 p.26]. The maintainer gives the same practice as "cornering", sitting at a table corner [@testimony-mullen-2026-10-05c cornering]. The rule fits family style. A family-style table sat eight, "seven other Wiessmen" and you [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6], each with a place to be served, so a ninth chair at the corner was for ordinary meals only. The clause about formal occasions outlived family style by fifteen years, and the entry outlived the clause. In 2026 the word for the custom survives; the reason it was once forbidden does not. The entry year by year is at [Corner](../glossary-series.md#corner).

## Square tables

The maintainer says Wiess is "the only college that has square tables to this day", and connects them with the old seated dinners [@testimony-mullen-2026-10-05c square tables] [T]. The corpus neither confirms nor contradicts the claim that the tables are unique. What it does show: the 2003 family-style table seated eight [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6], which is two to a side on a square; the 2017 photograph of the New Wiess Commons on the college website shows square tables set together in pairs, with chairs on all four sides [@wb 20170714224839 http://teamwiess.com/newstudents/rooms/commons.jpg]; and the 1990s Commons tables could be moved into a U for a Viking dinner [@riceinfo-norse]. No source describes the tables of the other colleges' dining halls. The present Commons furniture is on [The Commons and UpCo](../../places/commons.md#square-tables).

## As the college described it

!!! quote "O-Week Book 2003"
    "Family-style dining entails sitting down with seven other Wiessmen around a pseudo-fancy table, complete with a table cloth and real napkins, to be served by another student and eating from large serving dishes (like a family ideally does) instead of being boring and walking around a serving line with a cafeteria tray like the other, lesser colleges do. Before and during the meal our awesome Wiess secretary brings you announcements and cooks up some entertainment guaranteed to keep you amused while you await your delicious eats, served most often by fellow freshmen serving as waiters. Waiting is a great chance to do some random fun, cute stuff while meeting lots of other Wiessmen." [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]

!!! quote "O-Week Book 2003—glossary"
    "**Family Style.** 1. The faster, funner, better way that Wiessmen choose to eat. Once a week, we all sit and eat as a family instead of plodding through the kitchen line like the drones at other college. The night features entertainment, freshmen waiters, bonding and, if you're lucky, a ubangee." [@oweek-2003 conclusions p.3]

!!! quote "O-Week Book 2006"
    "Until two years ago, Wiess had a long tradition of family-style dinners every Monday through Thursday, where we relaxed and enjoyed dinner as a family. With big portions in the middle of the table, and friendly service from our smiling freshmen wait staff, family style was uniquely Wiess. But with our new building and beautiful servery, family style has become a thing of the past as quicker lines and more food choices made it a less-attractive option." [@oweek-2006 p.42]

!!! quote "O-Week Book 2014"
    "He instituted mandatory Wiess blazers (complete with crests) for formal Sunday dinners, where freshmen served upperclassmen a family-style dinner (a tradition that ended soon after the move to our current home) while sporting goldenrod and navy beanies to distinguish themselves (a tradition which also, thankfully, died out quickly)." [@oweek-2014 p.36]

## Variants & disputes

- **Formal Dinner, Sunday supper or family style?** The books keep two things apart that the maintainer's name joins. The "formal Sunday dinners" with blazers are a 1950s memory [@oweek-2006 p.37]; "family style" is the seated, served dinner of the weekdays, four nights a week until c.2004 [@oweek-2006 p.42] and in 2003 described as weekly [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]. The waiting was the same in both: freshmen served. "Formal Dinner" as a 2026 name may also borrow from today's Associates' Night, the only formal dinner the books still describe [@oweek-2025 p.26].
- **When it ended.** The books disagree with each other by a little: "lasted almost 50 years" (2006), "died out soon after the move to our current home" (2010) [@oweek-2006 p.37] [@oweek-2010 p.38]. The 2006 book's "Until two years ago" puts the end c.2004, two years after the move [@oweek-2006 p.42]. The 2003 book describes it as alive, and as weekly rather than four nights a week [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]. A Wiess alumnus blames "the forced advent of shared serveries" [@rhc 2013-03-15 friday-afternoon-follies-one-of-the-best-beanie-shots-of-all-times comment by George Webb, 21 Mar 2013]; the 2006 book blames quicker lines and more choice. Both describe the same building: South Servery, shared with Hanszen, opened with New Wiess (see [The Commons and UpCo](../../places/commons.md)). This closes the question left open on [Rules & Housing Rules](../../governance/rules.md#variants-disputes), which had the end "between 1998 and 2006".
- **Weekly or every weekday?** 1991: "seated weekday dinners" [@rules-1991 §VI]; late 1980s: "5 days a week" [@rhc 2013-03-15 friday-afternoon-follies-one-of-the-best-beanie-shots-of-all-times comment by George Webb, 21 Mar 2013]; 2003: "once a week" after O-Week [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]; 2006, looking back: "every Monday through Thursday" [@oweek-2006 p.42]. The 2003 description, written while it was happening, is the strongest witness for the last years; the shrinking from five nights to one fits a custom in decline in its new building.
- **Long tables.** The maintainer remembers long tables [@testimony-mullen-2026-10-05c Formal Dinner]. No written source describes the Old Wiess tables except as movable [@riceinfo-norse]; the 2003 family-style table seated eight [@oweek-2003 wiess p.6]. Square tables pushed together in a row make a long table, as the 2017 photograph shows [@wb 20170714224839 http://teamwiess.com/newstudents/rooms/commons.jpg], so the two memories may be of the same furniture.
- **Not the Gofer.** The 1994 glossary's "**Gofer.** The 'Fellows'' flunkies. You'll see them running around a lot during O-Week" [@handbook-1994] might be taken for a freshman servant; it is an O-Week helper, unconnected with waiting at dinner. The 1994 glossary has no entry for waiting, family style or the Headwaiter.
- **Beanie color.** "Green beanies" in 2003 [@oweek-2003 wiess p.2]; "goldenrod and navy beanies" from 2010 [@oweek-2010 p.38]. The beanie belongs on another page; it is noted here because the books tell it in the same sentence as the serving.

## Open questions

- When "Formal Dinner" came into use as a name, and whether it is the maintainer's generation's name for family style or for something else (a once-a-semester formal held after 2004?).
- The Headwaiter of 1991: elected, appointed, or a staff member [@rules-1991 §V]?
- Whether any other Rice college has square tables; a walk through the other commons would settle it in an afternoon.
- Photographs of family style in either building. The Campanile of the 1980s and 1990s is the likeliest source.
