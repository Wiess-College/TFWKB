# Court

The Wiess College Court is the college's elected disciplinary body. Every constitution since 1993 has given it the same purpose in the same words—"to insure [later 'ensure'] that the College is responsible for the self-discipline of its own members in College affairs"—and the same powers, "to investigate, to hold hearings and trials, to reprimand, and to produce decisions and sentences" under the University's judicial code [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §§1, 3] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §§1, 4]. It enforces the College Rules and may fine [@rules-1991 §VIII] [@constitution-2013 Art. IX]. Its composition has changed more than its job: five elected Associate Justices choosing a Chief Justice among themselves, with two junior Assistant Justices (1993–2013); three Deputy Justices (2016); and by 2026 a Chief Justice elected directly by the College, six Associate Justices and one Deputy Justice from the new student class, eight in all [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §2] [@constitution-2016 Art. IV §2] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §2]. In 2021 the reporting of students to Wiess Court for casual use of a racial slur was the occasion for the hate-speech clause now in Article XV, which the Court enforces "in partnership with the Diversity Facilitators" [@thresher-2021-09-15] [@constitution-2026 Art. XV §4]. The college's own description has softened from "maintaining some semblance of order" (1998) to "not a punitive force" (2021–23) [@riceinfo-court-1998] [@wiess-rice-edu-court-2023].

## What the constitutions say it is

**Purpose and authority.** The 1993 wording—"The Wiess College Court shall be conducted as stipulated in the Rice University Code of Judicial Procedure. The Court's purpose shall be to insure that the College is responsible for the self-discipline of its own members in College affairs"—survives in 2026 with "Code of Student Conduct" for "Code of Judicial Procedure" and "ensure" for "insure" [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §1] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §1]. The Court has always been able to refer matters up, and the Magister and a dean have always been able to take a matter away from it; the referral destinations change with University nomenclature—"the Master, University Court, Honor Council or Proctor" in 1993, "the University Court or the Judicial Affairs Committee" from 2007 [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §3] [@constitution-2007 Art. IV §3]. Misbehaviour by a Justice is tried "by the remainder of the Court" in every version [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §4]. From 2007 the Court must write its own "Wiess College Court Procedures and Code of Conduct", which the University's code supersedes; the vote to amend those procedures was four Justices until 2017 and "a simple majority" in 2026 [@constitution-2007 Art. IV §6] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §7]. No copy of the Court Procedures has been captured.

**Jurisdiction.** "Any complaint of an alleged violation of University Rules brought against any members of Wiess College" (1993); "an alleged Class II violation of University Rules… and students of other colleges violating Class II rules at Wiess College" (2007, 2013); Class II violations by Wiess members only (2016); "an alleged College Infraction of Rice University Student Code of Conduct (§ II.B.2)" (2026) [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §3] [@constitution-2007 Art. IV §3] [@constitution-2016 Art. IV §3] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §4]. The 2021 and 2023 web pages invite anyone to "Report a College Infraction" [@wiess-rice-edu-court-2023].

**Composition.** In 1993 the College elected five Associate Justices in the spring; they chose a Chief Justice among themselves within two weeks, "Seniority and past Court experience" to be taken into account; two Assistant Justices were "interviewed and recommended for the ballot by the currently presiding Court", elected in the fall, "non-voting, contributing members of the Court so as to prepare them for future Court positions", and took turns as Ombudsman [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §2]. In 2007 the Assistants became spring-elected, open to freshmen and voting [@constitution-2007 Art. IV §2 C]. In 2016 they became three "Deputy Justices", one reserved for a new student [@constitution-2016 Art. IV §2 C]. In 2026 the Chief Justice is for the first time elected directly—"The College shall vote for the Chief Justice by a majority"—and must be at least a second-year student; the Associate Justices number six and reach the ballot through "an application and/or interview process" run by the Chief Justice; there is one Deputy Justice, chosen within six weeks of the fall semester from the new students; and the Ombudsman is a standing post selected by the Associates by two-thirds, "The Chief Justice will not have a role of any capacity in the selection" [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §2]. The 2023 roster—one Chief Justice, six Associates, a Deputy "TBD" in September—already matches the 2026 shape, so the restructuring predates 2023 [@wiess-rice-edu-court-2023].

**Appeals.** A defendant could appeal "to the Wiess Court for review within two weeks, or… directly to the Master" from 1993 to 2013, and from 2016 only to the Magister [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §5] [@constitution-2016 Art. IV §5] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §6].

**Fines.** The Rules of 1991 are explicit that the Court fines: roof-climbers "will be fined, regardless of whether any damages to the tiles were made", freshmen who miss their waiting shift "shall be fined", and the schedule of penalties is set each spring by the incoming Chief Justice [@rules-1991 §§IV, VI, VIII]. The 1993 Treasury article caps fines at a limit "set annually by the Court and approved by a two-thirds vote of Cabinet"; from 2007 fines are simply "at the discretion of the Court" [@constitution-1993 Art. IX] [@constitution-2007 Art. IX] [@constitution-2026 Art. XII §4]. The 1999 Norse Night page remembers the Court's threat after the food-throwing of 1991 [@riceinfo-norse]. The 1993 Bylaws also gave Cabinet a say: "The Court shall recognize Cabinet dissatisfaction of an assessed penalty as a basis for reconsideration of that penalty"—a clause dropped by 2016 [@constitution-1993 Bylaws Art. V §10] [@constitution-2016 Bylaws Art. V].

**Procedure.** The hearing procedure—pleas of "Guilty" or "Not guilty", the witness sworn "to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth… and I will maintain secrecy about this trial", no character witnesses, the President sitting in when a Justice abstains—is word for word the same in the 1993 Bylaws and the 2026 Constitution, with the order of succession lengthened to run through "Treasurer, Secretary, SA Senator, Apprentice Treasurer, and Head Fellows" [@constitution-1993 Bylaws Art. V §§5–7] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §§12–14]. The abstract of "accusations, verdicts, and penalties of all court decisions, formal and informal" went to the Secretary of the Cabinet in 1993 and to the College Coordinator from 2016, who in 2026 publishes it with "only gender-neutral pronouns so as to avoid identification" [@constitution-1993 Bylaws Art. V §9] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §15].

## The 2021 hate-speech episode

On 15 September 2021 the Thresher reported that Wiess was adding a hate-speech clause to its constitution after students were reported to Wiess Court for casual use of a racial slur. President Varun Kukunoor said the clause "will have writing representation from the Wiess Cabinet, Court and A-Team"; Diversity Facilitator Alizay Azeem was also quoted; and Dean of Undergraduates Bridget Gorman noted that any college definition had to be consistent with University Policy 830 [@thresher-2021-09-15]. The 2026 text is the first version we hold with the result: Article XV defines hate speech by reference to Policy 830, says that violations "will face consequences as decided by the Wiess College Court" and are "enforceable by the Wiess College Court, regardless of intent", requires that proceedings "be conducted in partnership with the Diversity Facilitators", allows "determinations of restorative justice as advised by the Diversity Facilitators", directs reports "to the Chief Justice, any member of the Wiess College Court, or to a Diversity Facilitator", and refers bad-faith reporters to Student Judicial Programs [@constitution-2026 Art. XV §§2–7]. Whether the 2026 wording is the 2021–22 wording is not known.

## Variants & disputes

- **Five or four Associate Justices in 1998?** The Constitution called for five [@constitution-1993 Art. IV §2]; the July 1998 roster lists a Chief Justice and four Associates [@riceinfo-court-1998]. Either the Chief Justice is counted among the five (consistent with the Constitution, which has the Chief chosen from among the Associates) or a seat was vacant. The former reading is the more likely.
- **Who is ex officio?** The President (2007, 2013), the Resident Associates (2016, 2017), and both "The Resident Associates and the College President" (2026) [@constitution-2007 Art. IV §8] [@constitution-2016 Art. IV §8] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §9].
- **"Not a punitive force."** The 2021–23 self-description sits oddly beside a Constitution that still speaks of "trials", "sentences" and fines [@wiess-rice-edu-court-2023] [@constitution-2026 Art. VIII §4; Art. XII §4]. Both are the college's words; the page is tone, the Constitution is powers.

## Open questions

- No Court Procedures and Code of Conduct, required since 2007, has been captured. Ask the Chief Justice; the Court's own files should hold every version.
- No abstract of verdicts, required to be made public since 1993, has been captured.
- When did the Chief Justice become directly elected, and the Court grow to eight? Between 2017 and the 2023 roster [@constitution-2017] [@wiess-rice-edu-court-2023].
- What were the annual penalty schedules—the fine amounts—of the 1990s and 2000s? The Rules required them to be "posted in the commons and filed in the College office at all times" [@rules-1991 §VIII]; none is on the web.
- The 2021 cases: what the Court decided is private and should stay so; what the record needs is the date Cabinet adopted the clause.
